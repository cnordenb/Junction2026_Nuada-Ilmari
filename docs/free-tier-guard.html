How we catch a swarm of fake signups

Free plans attract bot networks that open hundreds of accounts for phishing, spam and abuse. One fake signup looks like a real person. A swarm gives itself away in the details its accounts share and in when they arrive. Hostinger closed its own free hosting brand, 000webhost, in 2024. This is the detector we built for that problem.

The pipeline

Every signup goes through the same four steps. Each step adds a reason in plain language, so a verdict always comes with the evidence behind it.

1

Check each signup on its own

Some signups give themselves away alone. Each check adds points and a sentence. At 60 points the signup is blocked; at 35 it goes to review.

Datacenter or VPN network“Privacy-minded people do this too, so on its own it is weak.”10
Device check skipped“The browser did not run the device check on the signup page.”10

A careful operator passes all of these, using real-looking addresses, a normal browser and residential proxies. That is why step 2 exists.

2

Link signups that share rare details

Two signups are linked when the details they share add up to a weight of 2 or more. Details that an operator has to fake count most. Network details count least, because offices, schools and VPN users share them innocently.

The 3% rule

A detail shared by more than 3% of all signups is not evidence and is ignored: gmail.com, this month's Chrome version, the country's biggest home-internet provider. Two strangers who both use Gmail on Chrome never link.

Email and username patterns

Letters become a, digits become 9 with their count. So marieesposito9124@outlook.com becomes a9{4}@outlook.com. A farm that generates names from a template shares one pattern. Real people mostly don't.

3

Ask whether the timing beats chance

Shared details alone can be innocent. So each group's signup times are compared with 4,000 random groups of the same size drawn from the same stream. A group only counts as synchronised if it beats the level that just 1 in 1,000 random groups reach. That keeps a busy evening from making everyone look coordinated.

Decide, and say why

Review
Looks like an organisation. Most of the group uses one school or company email domain, with no shared devices or inboxes. A class of 28 students on one campus IP and one Chromebook build lands here. We never block it.
Block
Most accounts already fail step 1 (average risk 40 or more).
Block
Shared identity details and unusual timing: a device, an inbox, an email pattern or an automation browser, with a timing score of 30 or more.
Block
Same network or browser build and strongly unusual timing (50 or more). The nightly batch from one rented server lands here.
Review
Weaker combinations. A person or a challenge page decides.
Allow
Everything else.

One more safeguard: inside a blocked group, an account is blocked only if it carries the group's evidence itself. An ordinary person who happened to link in through a looser match goes to review instead. Before we added this rule, one real person with the same email pattern as the nightly batch was blocked.

How well it works

We built a simulated signup stream with planted abuse, innocent look-alikes and an answer key. We designed against one stream and measured on five different ones. Then we measured on five hard streams, where the attackers use tricks we did not tune for: throwaway domains missing from our list, normal browsers, 20 devices instead of 4, and looser timing.

Where it fails, and what is not real yet

Misses

  • An operator who varies every recorded detail and signs up at ordinary times looks like real people to us. We do not claim to catch them.
  • A nightly batch spread over several hours drops from block to review.
  • Classes and company teams cost a human a look. We chose that over blocking a school.

Limits

  • All figures come from simulated signups that our own team wrote. They show the logic works as designed. They are not a measurement on a real platform.
  • The throwaway-email and hosting-network lists are small built-in snapshots. Production needs maintained lists and an IP-to-network database.
  • The live demo's device check is a simple hash of browser properties, and it has no IP-to-network lookup yet.

Nothing here identifies who is behind an account. The tool flags patterns and explains them, so a person can act on them.
